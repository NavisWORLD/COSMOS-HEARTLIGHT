# 🧩 HEARTLIGHT Classroom Scenarios

These examples teach the reasoning pattern. They are not treatment prescriptions.

## Scenario 1 — The worksheet never starts

**What the adult sees:** A worksheet is handed out. The learner looks away, taps the desk repeatedly, and does not begin.

**Old interpretation:** “Refusing work.”

**HEARTLIGHT questions:**

- Is the task visually overwhelming?
- Is the first step clear?
- Is movement needed before starting?
- Is the room noisier than usual?
- Would typing, pointing, or another response mode improve access?

**Low-risk supports to try:** one visible first step, two-minute movement break, quieter seat, alternate response format, first→then card.

**Measure:** Did access to the task improve?

---

## Scenario 2 — Covering ears during group work

**What the adult sees:** Learner covers ears and moves away during group discussion.

**Do not assume:** “They dislike peers.”

**Check:** sound load, social-space need, competing voices, room acoustics, communication demand.

**Possible supports:** more space, reduced group size, approved hearing protection, written participation, predictable turn-taking.

**Measure:** Can the learner participate with less distress?

---

## Scenario 3 — Transition escalation

**What the adult sees:** When recess ends, the learner drops to the floor and repeats “no.”

**HEARTLIGHT lens:** The transition itself may be the access barrier.

**Possible supports:** countdown, visual schedule, first→then, transition object, consistent return routine, choice of first indoor task.

**Measure:** Was the transition more predictable and less distressing?

---

## Scenario 4 — Fast repetitive movement

**What the adult sees:** Learner rocks quickly while listening to instruction.

**Do not assume:** that rocking means the learner cannot listen.

**Check:** Is the movement safe? Is the learner distressed? Can the learner still respond/participate? Do they want a movement option?

**Possible action:** leave safe stimming alone; provide more room or a safe movement seat if preferred.

**Measure:** participation and learner preference, not “looks typical.”

---

## Scenario 5 — “I don’t know” to every support question

**What the adult sees:** Learner repeatedly selects “I don’t know.”

**Possible meaning:** genuine uncertainty, processing delay, fatigue, too many choices, fear of choosing the “wrong” answer.

**Support:** reduce to two concrete choices or temporarily reduce demands.

**Measure:** whether communication becomes easier.

---

## Scenario 6 — Bright projector and headache-like behavior

**What the adult sees:** learner squints, turns away, rubs eyes, and stops looking at projected material.

**Ambient sensor:** reports a relatively bright environment.

**Important:** the sensor does not diagnose the cause.

**Possible supports:** ask learner, reduce screen brightness, change seat, use printed copy, reduce glare.

**Escalate appropriately:** if physical symptoms suggest a medical concern, follow school health procedures rather than relying on HEARTLIGHT.

---

## Scenario 7 — Communication is becoming smaller

**What the adult sees:** learner who usually talks begins giving one-word answers and looking down.

**HEARTLIGHT response:** offer communication alternatives without forcing speech: pointing, typing, yes/no, drawing, AAC.

**Measure:** whether the learner can communicate needs more easily.

---

## Scenario 8 — A support makes behavior quieter but learning worse

A learner is moved to an isolated desk. Vocal stimming stops, but the learner does not complete work and stops interacting.

**Important lesson:** quieter is not automatically better.

Evaluate access, communication, engagement, distress, autonomy, and learner preference. A support is not “effective” merely because it makes the environment easier for adults.
