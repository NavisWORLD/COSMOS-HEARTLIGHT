# HEARTLIGHT Teacher & Behavioral-Aide Manual

## 1. Purpose

HEARTLIGHT helps adults notice what a student may need before a difficult moment becomes a disciplinary story. It is designed for classrooms, resource rooms, libraries, after-school programs, transition programs, and other learning environments.

The tool is useful when a student:

- communicates needs indirectly or without speech;
- becomes overloaded by sound, light, crowding, uncertainty, or task complexity;
- seeks movement, pressure, sound, visual input, or repetition;
- needs predictable transitions;
- benefits from AAC, drawing, typing, pointing, or reduced language;
- appears disengaged but may actually be processing, regulating, or protecting themselves from overload.

## 2. The adult stance

Use three rules:

1. **Describe before interpreting.** Write what was seen/heard, not a motive.
2. **Support before punish.** Ask what environmental or communication barrier can be changed.
3. **Ask the learner.** A support is not successful merely because the room becomes quieter.

Example:

- Avoid: “Student was defiant and manipulative.”
- Prefer: “When the worksheet was placed on the desk, the student pushed it away, covered their ears, and said ‘no’ three times. The room was loud during group transition.”

## 3. Five-minute classroom loop

### Perceive
Observe the environment, task, communication channel, and body/sensory cues.

### Compress
Summarize the situation in neutral language: “high noise + transition + multi-step demand.”

### Expand
Generate several possible supports instead of one explanation: quieter location, first/then card, reduced words, movement, task chunking, AAC, processing time.

### Validate
Offer choices and check what the learner communicates. Do not assume that compliance equals regulation.

### Express
State the next step simply and respectfully.

### Store
Record only what is useful. Avoid unnecessary names, camera images, health details, or permanent labels.

## 4. HEARTLIGHT child screen

The child screen is a communication board, not a test. A learner can select:

- quiet;
- movement;
- less light;
- more space;
- help;
- break;
- pressure/proprioceptive option;
- sound choice;
- predictability;
- another communication method;
- “I don’t know”;
- “stay with me.”

Do not force a child to select a need. A blank screen is data too: they may not want to interact, may need time, or may prefer another communication system.

## 5. Observation notes

HEARTLIGHT uses a simple context -> observable communication/behavior -> support -> result structure. This resembles functional observation but does **not** replace a formal functional behavioral assessment (FBA) performed under applicable school procedures.

Good observation notes answer:

- What was happening immediately before?
- What did the student visibly/audibly do?
- What support was offered?
- What changed next?
- What did the student communicate about the support?

## 6. Positive behavioral support

Current U.S. Department of Education IDEA resources emphasize positive, proactive behavioral supports and note that IEP teams must consider positive behavioral interventions and supports when behavior impedes learning. HEARTLIGHT is designed to support that human process, not replace it.

References:
- https://sites.ed.gov/idea/behavioral-support-resources/
- https://sites.ed.gov/idea/idea-files/osep-dear-colleague-letter-on-ensuring-equity-and-providing-behavioral-supports-to-students-with-disabilities/
- https://sites.ed.gov/idea/idea-files/guide-positive-proactive-approaches-to-supporting-children-with-disabilities/

## 7. What HEARTLIGHT may suggest

Low-risk examples include:

- lower competing noise;
- reduce glare;
- visual schedule or first/then cue;
- smaller task step;
- safe movement break;
- more processing time;
- choice of communication mode;
- change of seat/location;
- familiar, consensual sensory support;
- nearby adult presence with fewer words.

These are options, not prescriptions.

## 8. What HEARTLIGHT must never decide

Do not use it to determine:

- disability or diagnosis;
- whether a student is “dangerous,” “manipulative,” or “noncompliant”;
- discipline;
- restraint or seclusion;
- medication or medical treatment;
- special-education eligibility;
- IEP placement or service minutes;
- whether a student is telling the truth;
- whether a student is ready to return from a break;
- whether harmless stimming should stop.

## 9. Team review

Use records to ask questions, not to manufacture certainty.

A weekly team review can ask:

1. What contexts repeatedly precede overload?
2. Which supports did the learner seek or accept?
3. Which supports were followed by better access to learning or communication?
4. What support attempts did not help?
5. What can be changed in the environment instead of demanding that the child change?
6. Does the learner agree with the adults' interpretation?

## 10. Emergency boundary

If a learner may be injured, is acutely ill, or there is an immediate safety emergency, follow the school's emergency procedures and qualified professional guidance. Do not rely on HEARTLIGHT sensor readings.
