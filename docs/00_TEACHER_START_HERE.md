# 👩‍🏫 HEARTLIGHT Teacher Start Here

This is the shortest path from “I downloaded the repo” to “I can responsibly try this in a classroom.”

## First: what HEARTLIGHT is for

HEARTLIGHT helps a student communicate support needs and helps an adult organize low-risk accommodations and neutral observations.

It is useful when a learner may:

- need movement but cannot explain why
- become overloaded by sound, light, crowding, or transitions
- communicate differently from peers
- use AAC, typing, gesture, pointing, pictures, or limited speech
- shut down or become hard to read
- mask distress
- need more processing time
- stim or seek repetitive sensory input
- have trouble getting started even when they understand the work
- need adults to change the environment rather than simply repeat a demand

It is not a diagnostic test and should never be used to determine whether a child “has” a disability.

## Before using it with students

1. Ask your school/district whether the tool is approved for classroom use.
2. Review `PRIVACY_AND_SAFETY.md`.
3. Decide whether you need to save observations at all.
4. If you do save observations, use a school-approved non-identifying student code rather than a name whenever possible.
5. Do not enter medical diagnoses, private family information, IEP text, or detailed protected information into a personal/unmanaged device.
6. If you use the camera sensor, point it at the **room/light source**, not the child.
7. Keep existing IEP, 504, behavior, safety, communication, health, and crisis plans in force.

## The 5-minute learner setup

Open the **Child** screen.

Say something simple such as:

> “This gives you another way to tell me what might help. You can choose more than one thing. ‘I don’t know’ is okay.”

Do not require the learner to pick an option. Do not turn it into a quiz.

The learner can select needs like quiet, movement, more space, a break, help, less light, another way to communicate, predictability, or staying near a trusted adult.

## The HEARTLIGHT loop

### 1. Ask
Give the learner a low-pressure chance to communicate.

### 2. Listen
Treat the selection as information, not proof.

If the learner presses **Quiet**, that means “consider quiet.” It does not prove a clinical condition or tell you exactly why the learner needs quiet.

### 3. Offer
Offer one or two realistic options.

Bad pattern:

> “You pressed break, so you have to go to the break area.”

Better pattern:

> “You picked break. Would you rather sit here quietly or use the calm area?”

### 4. Reduce language
When someone is overloaded, more explanation is not always more helpful.

Try:

- one visible next step
- first → then
- two choices rather than ten
- a visual timer
- extra processing time

### 5. Observe
Use language a camera could approximately record.

Instead of:

> “He was manipulative.”

Write:

> “Worksheet was placed on desk. Student pushed it approximately 30 cm away, covered ears, and looked toward hallway.”

### 6. Check the result
Ask the learner if possible.

Also observe whether access improved:

- did breathing/movement settle?
- did communication increase?
- did the learner approach the task?
- did they use the break safely?
- did another support work better?

Do not call a support “effective” just because behavior became quieter.

### 7. Return with dignity
A break needs a path back that is predictable and non-shaming.

Examples:

- “Two more minutes, then we check together.”
- “When you’re ready, your first step is just writing your name.”
- “You can come back with headphones.”

## When the learner chooses “I don’t know”

Treat it as communication.

“I don’t know” may reflect:

- processing time
- too many simultaneous signals
- difficulty identifying internal sensations
- uncertainty about what adults will allow
- fatigue
- fear of being wrong
- simply not knowing

Try reducing demands temporarily and offering two concrete support choices.

## Stimming

Do not automatically interrupt harmless repetitive movement or sound just because it looks unusual.

Ask:

1. Is anyone unsafe?
2. Is the learner distressed?
3. Is the environment blocking participation more than the stim itself?
4. Can we make the stim safer or easier rather than remove it?
5. Does the learner want help changing it?

Use `STIMMING_GUIDE.md` for the full framework.

## Color / light sensor

The sensor estimates room RGB and brightness.

Use it as a **prompt to check**, not as a prescription.

A bright reading can justify asking:

- “Is the light bothering you?”
- “Would another seat help?”
- “Would you like the screen dimmer?”

It does not mean every learner needs a darker room.

## Teacher notes

HEARTLIGHT stores notes locally only when you explicitly save them.

A useful entry has four parts:

```text
Context:
Independent writing started after lunch; projector on; room louder than usual.

Observable behavior:
Student covered ears, rocked faster, and did not begin writing for five minutes.

Support offered:
Headphones or quieter table; student selected quieter table.

Observable result:
Student sat at quieter table and began typing after approximately two minutes.
```

That entry describes what happened without claiming to know the student's motive.

## Stop and use established procedures when

HEARTLIGHT is not a crisis tool. Follow your school’s trained procedures and the learner’s established safety/health plan when there is:

- immediate danger
- suspected medical emergency
- severe self-injury
- threat of serious harm
- elopement into a dangerous area
- concern about abuse or neglect
- any situation requiring mandated reporting or emergency response

## Your first week

### Day 1
Use only the Child communication screen and visual timer.

### Day 2
Introduce one sensory/environmental support choice.

### Day 3
Practice neutral observation notes with **no student identifiers**.

### Day 4
Review whether the learner actually preferred the supports offered.

### Day 5
Discuss with the learner/team what should stay, change, or be removed.

## The teacher test

Before using any HEARTLIGHT output, ask:

> **Does this increase the learner’s access, communication, autonomy, safety, or dignity?**

If the answer is no, the software suggestion does not belong in the plan.
