# HEARTLIGHT Stimming & Sensory Regulation Guide

## Purpose

“Stimming” is a common informal term for repetitive movement, sound, sensory interaction, or other repetition. HEARTLIGHT does not treat stimming as a behavior category that must be eliminated.

The first questions are:

1. Is anyone in immediate physical danger?
2. Is the action causing injury or significant physical harm?
3. Is the learner communicating a need through it?
4. Is the environment creating overload?
5. Does the learner want help changing or redirecting it?

If the stim is safe, the default is **accommodation and curiosity**, not suppression.

## Support families

### Movement
Examples: rocking, pacing, bouncing, stretching, hand movement.

Possible supports: movement breaks, safe pacing route, standing option, rocking/alternative seat if appropriate, wall pushes, carrying ordinary classroom materials.

### Tactile
Examples: rubbing textures, manipulating objects, tapping.

Possible supports: permitted fidget, textured object, fabric patch, putty where safe and age-appropriate.

### Auditory / vocal
Examples: humming, repeating phrases, rhythmic sound.

Possible supports: quieter location, agreed sound level, headphones/ear protection where appropriate, alternate space for vocalization.

### Visual
Examples: watching movement, pattern repetition, looking at light/reflections.

Possible supports: controlled visual object, reduced glare, visual break, seating that limits overwhelming visual traffic.

### Proprioceptive / pressure-seeking
Possible supports: pushing against a wall, resistance movement, carrying a light object, or other familiar options recommended by the learner's team.

**Never impose deep pressure, holding, restraint, or touch because the software suggested “pressure.” Consent and the student's established plan matter.**

### Oral-motor
Schools should follow hygiene, choking-safety, age-appropriateness, and professional plans for chewable or oral sensory tools. HEARTLIGHT does not prescribe them.

## Redirection without shame

If a stim is unsafe or prevents the learner from accessing an essential activity, try to preserve its function while changing the form.

Instead of: “Stop doing that.”

Use: “I want to help you keep your body safe. Do you want this movement option or this one?”

## Never use HEARTLIGHT to

- score how “normal” a child appears;
- reward masking;
- force eye contact;
- suppress harmless repetitive movement for adult convenience;
- infer intent from a stim;
- diagnose autism, ADHD, anxiety, sensory-processing conditions, or any other condition.
